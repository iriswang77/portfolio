Product case study · MVP delivered

An AI knowledge assistant for 3,000+ employees who needed answers, not documents

A retrieval-augmented assistant for employee training in a production-planning department of a German automotive OEM in China. I owned the whole chain: interviews, product definition, prototype, PRD, RAG pipeline, and the interface contract that keeps a probabilistic system honest.

01 / CONTEXT

The problem was not a missing document. It was a missing answer.

New joiners in the training function spent an average of 45 minutes locating a single answer inside several hundred pages of training material. When they did ask a colleague, a single question consumed about 15 minutes of that colleague's working time. The content existed. The path to it did not.

Why this mattered

The training team reached 3,000+ employees per year. At that scale, "find the document" stops being a personal inconvenience and becomes an organisational tax — paid in onboarding time, in expert time diverted from real work, and in answers that never arrive.

What the request actually was

The brief I received was framed as a knowledge-management tool: build a system over the documentation. My first job was to argue that this framing was wrong. A document search tool optimises the wrong moment — the one where someone already knows what they need and only lacks the pointer. Training questions are different: the asker usually does not yet know what the right question is.

So I reframed the objective before writing any specification: not "find the document", but "close the question" — and that reframing decided the architecture, the interface, and the definition of success that followed.

02 / RESEARCH & PRODUCT DEFINITION

Finding the real question behind the question

Before designing the assistant, I had to find out what people were actually asking when they said they were "looking something up". This was the part of the work that shaped the product most.

STEP 01

Interviews with the askers

I interviewed new joiners and training staff to map what they were really trying to achieve, not just which keywords they typed. Most "document" questions turned out to be procedural — "what do I do next" — and those have no single keyword answer.

STEP 02

Personas & positioning

I segmented users by role and question type rather than by seniority, because the two questions the system gets asked differ by workflow, not by age. Each persona carries a distinct intent class, and intent class is what drives the retrieval path.

STEP 03

Function list, flow & PRD

From the personas I derived the feature list, the user flow and a written PRD — including the acceptance criteria the retrieval layer had to satisfy before the interface could ship.

The research finding that shaped the architecture

One pattern came up repeatedly and it was not about content volume. New joiners did not need more documentation; they needed to know whether the answer they were given was safe to act on. In their words: they could not tell the difference between a confident answer and a confident-sounding wrong answer, because the source material they had was too long to verify.

That finding is why the system I built has a confidence model, a source trail on every answer, and an explicit "I don't know" path — instead of a chat box that always produces a paragraph.

If the assistant is wrong, the cost is not a bad sentence. It is a new employee acting on a confident error in a production environment. Design for that, not for the demo.
03 / SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

A pipeline built around intent, not a search box

The system I specified and built routes every question through four stages. The critical design decision sits in stage one: classifying the question type before deciding how to answer it, rather than retrieving first and interpreting afterwards.

Why a dynamic workflow instead of one fixed chain

The retrieval corpus mixes policy documents, step-by-step procedures, comparison tables and illustrated instructions. A single retrieval-then-generate chain handles the middle of that range well and the edges badly. Routing by intent lets a procedural question pull the step sequence in order, a factual question pull a short cited excerpt, and a comparative question pull two sources side by side and reconcile them.

The same routing layer also handles language auto-detection, so a user can ask in the language they think in and still receive an answer in the language of the source material — which matters when the corpus itself is multilingual.

Grounding as a product feature

"Grounding" here is not an implementation detail; it is the part of the product a user actually experiences. Every answer carries the passage it came from, image evidence where the source contained a diagram, and a follow-up suggestion so the conversation has a next step instead of a dead end. Short-term memory keeps the thread coherent across turns.

04 / THE HUMAN‑AI CONTRACT

Four states the interface has to support — and what the user is owed in each

This is the part of the work I would put in front of a design review first. An assistant that only handles the happy path is not a product. These four states are the actual surface area, and each one carries an explicit obligation to the user.

Confident answerSTATE 01

Retrieval was strong and the answer is grounded. The interface states the answer directly, shows a confidence level, and attaches the source passage with image evidence where relevant.

Offered next: two or three follow-up questions so the user can move on without re-typing a query.

Owed to the user: speed, and the ability to verify in one click.
Partial answer → guided follow-upSTATE 02

Retrieval found relevant material but not enough to answer. Rather than guessing, the assistant narrows the question — offering the plausible interpretations as a short list to disambiguate.

New joiner vs. existing employee?
Domestic travel vs. international?
Standard vs. expedited approval?
Owed to the user: a cheap way to repair the question, not a wrong answer.
Low confidence → visible uncertaintySTATE 03

Evidence is thin. The answer states plainly that it is not confident, shows what it did find, and marks the claim as unverified instead of presenting it as fact. Uncertainty is displayed, not smoothed over.

Owed to the user: to know the difference before acting.
Knowledge gap → routed to a humanSTATE 04

The corpus genuinely does not cover it. The system does not improvise — it files a gap report with the question, what was searched and what was missing, so content owners can fix the source and the next person gets a real answer.

This is the loop that turns usage into content improvement.

Owed to the user: an honest "not yet" — and a way to get it fixed.

The design rule underneath all four

A language model will always be able to produce a fluent answer. The designer's job is to make the interface resist the temptation to always produce one. Every state above is a place where the product is allowed to be incomplete, and each one tells the user exactly how incomplete it is.

The same logic drove the front end: a zero-friction HTML interface rather than a tool people have to be trained on. In a training context, an interface that requires learning is an interface that will not be used.

05 / TRADE-OFFS I MADE

Two designs I rejected — and why

The most useful thing I can show about a product is not only the thing I shipped, but the alternatives I understood well enough to turn down. These were the two real forks in the road.

Rejected Fixed FAQ list + search box

The cheaper build, and the one the brief asked for

A static list of the 200 most-asked questions, each with a curated written answer. It is faster to build, trivially accurate, and completely bypasses the model.

It also fails the moment a question is not on the list, which is most of a new joiner's actual experience — they do not yet know what the FAQ contains, so they cannot search for it. It solves the expert's question ("which question is answered?") rather than the beginner's ("I don't know where to start?").

Why I rejected it: it optimises for the retrieval of known questions. The population with the sharpest need is the one least able to name the question. The cost was accepted because the routing layer lets the system handle known and unknown question types in one path.

Rejected Pure chat interface, answer as a paragraph

The most familiar pattern, and the one users expect

A clean chat transcript. The model answers in fluent prose, and the conversation feels natural and fast — the demo looks better than anything else in the room.

But prose is exactly the format in which a wrong answer is most persuasive, and it is the format that hides the provenance of the answer. A user reading a paragraph has no way to know which sentence came from the policy and which was generated.

Why I rejected it: it optimises for the first thirty seconds of the interaction and leaves the user unequipped for the moment they act on the answer. The answer card with a confidence level and an attached source passage is less elegant and far more defensible — and defensibility is what the use case requires.

A note on the limits of the MVP: the assistant was delivered as a working MVP, not a mature product. Enterprise platform integrations with the wider document landscape were scoped as a later milestone rather than shipped in v1 — a deliberate choice to keep the grounding quality high rather than widen the corpus before the confidence model was trustworthy.
06 / OUTCOME

What changed

15 min → seconds
Average time to resolve a training question, versus the previous manual-handling baseline.
45 min → ~1 min
Time to locate a specific answer inside several hundred pages of training material.
3,000+
Employees covered annually by the training function that this assistant serves.
>90% target
Target answer-adoption rate set for the MVP — a defined acceptance criterion rather than a measured result.

Beyond the metric

The number I would put in front of a user is the second one: 45 minutes to about a minute. The first metric (15 minutes to seconds) matters because it converts expert time back into training capacity, which is the resource that was actually scarce.

I am deliberately reporting the adoption figure as a target. The MVP was delivered and validated; the measured adoption rate over sustained use was the next measurement, not one I can quote today. Separating "we set this bar" from "we cleared this bar" is the difference between a portfolio claim and a marketing claim, and only the first one is worth anything in an interview.

What I would do next

  • Measure the states, not the traffic. The interface instruments four states, but what I actually want is the distribution between them — how often users land in the guided-follow-up state, and how often the system routes to a human. That distribution is the real quality metric of a retrieval system.
  • Close the loop in the other direction. The gap report tells content owners what is missing. The same data should tell them which of their existing documents nobody ever reaches, which is a more embarrassing and more actionable finding.
  • Make the confidence level explainable. A percentage tells the user how much to trust the answer. It does not tell them why, or what would change it. The next step is turning the confidence breakdown into a sentence a person can act on.
07 / REFLECTION

What this project taught me about designing with AI

1. The design problem moved from the interface to the contract. With a deterministic system, the interface has to be right about input. With a probabilistic one, the interface has to be right about epistemics — how confident the system is, what it is based on, and what it does not know. That is a different craft, and it is closer to information design than to screen design.

2. The most valuable output of the research was a constraint, not a feature. "Show the source and show the confidence" came directly from one interview finding, and it changed the architecture more than any other single decision in the project.

3. "Unhappy paths" are the actual product. For a document search, the happy path is a search. For an AI assistant, the happy path is roughly one of four states. Designing only the confident answer would have produced something impressive in a demo and unusable in a plant.
